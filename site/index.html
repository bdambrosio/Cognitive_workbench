Who it is for

Developers choosing between open-source projects, where the README is most of what you have to go on. Advisers and buyers of software businesses in the $1M to $5M range, at the point where the seller's description of the product is the main thing on the table. A claims review sits a rung below a full technical due diligence: narrower, quicker, and priced so it can be done before the letter of intent rather than after.

It is not a penetration test, not a review of code quality or architecture, and not legal advice. It reports the state of each claim and what a gap would change for you.

How it works

01
Intake.

A short conversation about what you are evaluating and why: what you already know, what would change your mind, and, for a deal, what would move the price or end it.

02
Review.

The claims are listed and frozen before any evidence is read, and then each one is tested against the code and the documents supplied.

03
Check and rate.

A second, independent pass re-reads the evidence behind every finding. Each gap is rated against your thresholds, twice, and looked at again when the two readings differ.

04
Report and questions.

You receive the report and a private page where you can ask about any finding in your own time, with the working record behind every answer.

Why it holds up

The reading is done by language models, but not by asking one a question and taking its word. The models work inside a fixed procedure that keeps the list of claims separate from the verdicts, keeps every search and every file opened, and refuses a finding it cannot cite. A person reads the result and stands behind it. The report says on every line which part was machine and which was judgment.

Machine drafts. A person confirms and signs. The report tells you which is which.
